Shadow highlight is a **Colour** effect.

## What it is for

Someone standing in front of a window comes out as a silhouette: the camera
exposed for the window, and the face went to nothing. Lifting the whole picture
would blow the window out. This lifts the dark *regions* and pulls down the
bright ones, and leaves the rest alone.

The word doing the work is **regions**. Whether a pixel is treated as a shadow
is decided by how bright its *surroundings* are, not how bright it is — so a
white shirt button inside a dark jacket is lifted along with the jacket instead
of being singled out and left behind. That is what makes the result look like a
better exposure rather than a washed-out one.

Everyday uses: rescuing a backlit interview, opening up crushed blacks in
graded footage, holding a blown sky, and putting a bit of local contrast into a
flat log clip.

## What each control does

- **Shadow amount** — how hard the dark regions are lifted. 100 trebles them.
- **Shadow tonal width** — how far up the tone range counts as shadow. Low keeps
  the lift in the deepest darks; high reaches into the midtones and starts to
  look like an overall brightening.
- **Highlight amount** — how hard the bright regions are pulled down. 100 takes
  them to a third.
- **Highlight tonal width** — how far down the range counts as highlight.
- **Radius** — how large a neighbourhood decides whether a pixel is in shadow.
  Small values pick out local contrast and can look gritty; large values behave
  more like an overall tone curve. It is a per cent of the composition diagonal,
  so it survives a change of resolution.
- **Colour correction** — how much saturation is put back where the picture
  moved. Opening a shadow reads as desaturated; this is the cure. 0 leaves
  colour exactly alone.
- **Midtone contrast** — the contrast the two lifts flatten, put back by hand.
  0 is neutral; a little positive is usually worth having after a strong rescue.
- **Mix** — how much of the corrected picture replaces the original. This is
  where an imported After Effects "Blend with original" lands.
- **Matte** and **Invert** — correct only part of the frame; see
  [Effects](/use/effects/).

## Notes

**Nothing is softened.** The effect looks at a blurred copy of the picture to
decide what is a shadow, but it never takes any colour from it — the blur is a
question, not an answer. Detail comes out exactly as sharp as it went in.

**If you see a halo, lower Radius or lower the amounts.** A bright ring around a
dark object against a bright background is the signature of a local tone
correction pushed too far, and it is the one artefact to watch for. The default
pair is deliberately mild.

**Amounts at 0 turn the effect off exactly**, and it does not even do the blur,
so a disabled instance costs nothing.

**There is no automatic mode.** After Effects picks its amounts from the frame's
own histogram and then smooths that choice across neighbouring frames, which
makes a grade whose answer at one frame depends on the shot around it. Lumit's
amounts are yours, and stay where you put them; an imported project arrives with
After Effects' default pair written in.

**For a global lift rather than a local one**, use
[Levels](/effects/colour/levels/), [Curves](/effects/colour/curves/) or
[Exposure](/effects/colour/exposure/) — they are cheaper and more predictable
when the whole picture needs the same treatment.
