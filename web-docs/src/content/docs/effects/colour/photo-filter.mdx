Photo filter is a **Colour** effect.

## What it is for

Before white balance was a menu, a photographer screwed a coloured glass onto
the front of the lens to warm a shot up or cool it down. This is that glass.
Twenty named filters ship — the warming and cooling filters photographers know
by their numbers, eight plain colours, sepia, four deep ones and an underwater —
plus **Custom**, which takes any colour you like.

Reach for it when a shot is too blue or too orange and you want the fix to be
one control rather than three, or when you want a whole montage to sit under one
consistent cast.

## What each control does

- **Filter** — which glass. The three **warming** filters take a blue cast off
  and are the ones to reach for on an overcast exterior; the three **cooling**
  filters take an orange cast off and suit tungsten interiors. The plain colours
  and the deep ones are for effect rather than correction.
- **Colour** — the glass's own colour, used when Filter is set to Custom. It is
  scene-linear, so picking a colour from a reference frame with the eyedropper
  gives you that frame's cast.
- **Density** — how strong the glass is. **0** turns the effect off exactly; 25
  is a gentle correction; 100 is the full filter, which on a deep colour is very
  strong indeed.
- **Preserve luminosity** — put the exposure back after the filter. On, the
  picture changes colour and not brightness. Off, the filter costs light exactly
  as a real one does, which is what you want if you are simulating the glass
  rather than correcting with it.
- **Mix** — how much of the filtered picture replaces the original. Density and
  Mix do similar things at low values; use Density for the strength of the glass
  and Mix to blend the whole effect back.
- **Matte** and **Invert** — filter only part of the frame; see
  [Effects](/use/effects/).

## Notes

**Preserve luminosity is what makes a filter a filter.** With it off, a deep red
at full density is nearly a black frame, because a red glass really does stop
most of the light. With it on, the brightness you started with is restored, so
you are choosing a colour rather than an exposure. It is on by default.

**The twenty named filters are Lumit's own colours under the familiar names.**
The designations — 85, LBA, 81, 80, LBB, 82 — are the photographic ones and mean
what they always have; the exact chromaticities are ours, so an imported After
Effects project converts look for look rather than value for value.

**For a warmth control with a temperature scale**, use
[Temperature](/effects/colour/temperature/), which thinks in kelvin.
Photo filter thinks in glass.
