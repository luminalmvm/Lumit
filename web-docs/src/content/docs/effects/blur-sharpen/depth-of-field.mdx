Depth of field is a [Blur & sharpen](/effects/blur-sharpen/) effect.

## What it is for

A blur whose strength changes per pixel, driven by a **depth pass** — a second
layer whose brightness says how far away each pixel is. Pixels at the focus
distance stay sharp and everything ahead of or behind that plane softens, the
way a real lens throws a background out of focus.

This is the effect that makes a game capture read as photographed rather than
rendered. Engine footage is sharp everywhere, which is the single most reliable
tell; a depth pass exported alongside it, driven through here, buys back the
whole look. It is also how a subject is separated from a busy background without
keying anything.

Two things make it read as a lens rather than as a smudge, and both are
adjustable. The blur is gathered through a **polygon**, not a circle, because a
real iris has blades — which is why an out-of-focus highlight is a hexagon on
some lenses and a disc on others. And bright points are averaged in a way that
lets them survive their dark surroundings and bloom into balls, instead of
dissolving into grey.

You need a depth layer for any of this. Bring the depth pass in as an ordinary
layer, hide it so it does not render into the composition, and point **Depth
layer** at it. Until you do, the effect does nothing at all and says so. If you
have no depth pass, [Gaussian blur](/effects/blur-sharpen/gaussian-blur/) with a
mask is the honest alternative.

The defocused picture replaces the original rather than being added over it. To
lay bokeh over a sharp plate, put the effect on an adjustment layer and give the
layer a blend mode — see [Blend modes](/use/blend-modes/).

## What each control does

The picture, and where focus sits:

- **Depth layer** — the layer read as the depth pass. It only has to exist in the
  composition and cover the frame in time; hide it, since a depth map should not
  appear in the picture. Unset or pointing at a layer that has gone, the whole
  effect steps aside.
- **Focus distance** — which depth stays sharp, read against the depth pass's own
  zero-to-one range. This is the control you animate to rack focus from one
  subject to another.
- **Use focus point** and **Focus point x** / **Focus point y** — the alternative
  to hunting for a number. Turn the switch on and click the thing you want
  sharp; the effect reads the depth under that spot and focuses there. Focus
  distance greys out while it is on, and the point pair greys out while it is
  off, because only one of them can be in charge.
- **Focus range** — the half-width of the band around focus that stays fully
  sharp before softening begins. Widen it to keep a whole subject crisp rather
  than one plane through it.
- **Aperture** — the master scale on both blur sizes. This is the one to reach
  for first: it moves near and far together, the way opening up a real lens does.
- **Near blur** and **Far blur** — the maximum softening on each side of focus,
  in pixels. Real lenses are not symmetric about focus, and neither is taste:
  most shots want the background thrown much further than the foreground.

The **Iris** group shapes each out-of-focus highlight:

- **Blades** — how many straight edges the aperture has. It does nothing while
  Roundness is fully round, since a circle has no blades.
- **Roundness** — fully round is a disc, zero is the straight-edged polygon, and
  negative pulls the edges inward so the shape becomes a star. Five blades at
  full negative is the classic starred bokeh.
- **Rotation** — turns the polygon. It winds through whole turns, so it can be
  animated to spin.
- **Aspect ratio** — squeezes the highlight oval. Positive stretches it wide and
  negative tall; this is the anamorphic look, a squeeze either side of round.
- **Rim brightness** — where the light sits inside each ball. A real lens rarely
  throws a flat disc: negative pools the light in the middle for creamy bokeh,
  zero is the flat disc, and positive rings the edge bright, the soap-bubble
  look.

The **Highlights** group decides what blooms:

- **Highlight threshold** — the brightness a pixel must pass to be treated as a
  highlight rather than as ordinary picture.
- **Highlight exposure** — how hard those highlights bloom into balls. At zero
  the whole split is skipped and the blur is a plain average, which is the
  cheaper and flatter result.

The **Depth map** group is about reading the pass, as distinct from where focus
sits:

- **Depth channel** — which channel carries the depth. Luminance suits the grey
  map a depth pass usually is; alpha and the individual colour channels are
  there for passes packed another way.
- **Depth invert** — swaps near and far. Depth passes disagree on which end is
  close, and this is the switch for when yours is the other way round.
- **Gamma** — spreads the depth out before it is read. This matters more than it
  sounds: a linear depth channel puts the sky at the far end and compresses an
  entire room into the bottom of the range, so without it focus is
  all-or-nothing. Each step doubles the spread, and game footage usually wants a
  good deal of it — the middle of the slider rather than either end.
- **Remove edge leak** and **Detect edge threshold** — suppress the halo where a
  sharp near object meets a blurred far one and the blur reaches across the
  boundary for colour it should not have. The threshold decides what counts as
  such a boundary.

And the tail:

- **Repeat edge pixels** — holds the outermost pixel where the gather reaches
  past the frame, rather than reading transparency and darkening the border.
- **Display** — a diagnostic view. Depth map shows what the effect actually reads
  after the channel pick and the invert, and Focus map shows white where the
  picture is being kept sharp. Reach for these when focus lands somewhere you
  did not expect; they are far quicker than reasoning about the numbers.
- **Mix** — how much of the defocused picture replaces the original.
