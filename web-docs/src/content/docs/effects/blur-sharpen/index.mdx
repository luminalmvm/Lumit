## What this family does

Softening a picture, and its opposite. Six effects share one job description and
divide it by *shape*: what path each pixel's light is spread along.

- **Gaussian blur** spreads it evenly in every direction. It is the plain
  softening you reach for by default.
- **Directional blur** spreads it along one angle, so the picture streaks. This
  is the impact-frame and speed-line tool.
- **Radial blur** spreads it around, or away from, a chosen centre — a spin or a
  punch. Put the centre on the subject and the frame appears to rush at it.
- **Depth of field** varies the amount per pixel, driven by a depth pass, so near
  and far soften while the focus plane stays sharp. It is the only one that
  needs a second layer.
- **Unsharp mask** and **Sharpen** run the other way, lifting detail rather than
  dissolving it.

The four blurring radii — Gaussian blur's and Unsharp mask's **Radius**,
Directional blur's **Length**, Radial blur's **Amount** — are percentages of the
composition diagonal rather than counts of pixels, so a blur survives a
composition resize and a draft preview looks like the export, only softer.

Two exceptions are worth knowing. **Depth of field** authors its near and far
blur in pixels, because a lens's circle of confusion is a real physical size;
those still follow the preview resolution, so a draft matches. **Sharpen**'s
Radius is different in kind: it is a step between neighbouring pixels of the
frame as it is actually being drawn, not a length at composition size, so it
deliberately does not scale with a reduced-resolution preview. On a Quarter
scrub the same setting therefore lands on a coarser scale relative to the
picture than it will on export. Judge Sharpen at full resolution.

Blurring is not as expensive as it is elsewhere. A wide radius costs close to
what a narrow one costs, so there is no reason to hold back on the number.

### Blur, or sharpen, in which order

The stack runs top to bottom, so a blur above a sharpen and a sharpen above a
blur are different pictures. Sharpening after a blur claws back some of what the
blur removed; sharpening before it simply gives the blur more to smear. See
[Effects](/use/effects/).
