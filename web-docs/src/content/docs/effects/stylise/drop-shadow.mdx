Drop shadow is a **Stylise** effect.

## What it is for

Lifting a layer off whatever is behind it. The layer already carries its shape in
its transparency — the solid parts, the soft edge, every scrap of antialiasing —
and this blurs that shape, tints it, slides it in the direction the light comes
from, and draws it **underneath** the layer.

That "underneath" is the whole reason it is an effect rather than a duplicated
layer: one instance gives you the shadow and the thing casting it, correctly
stacked, in the right place, animated together.

It is the most-reached-for effect in motion graphics for a reason. Titles,
lower thirds, logos, UI panels, cut-out photographs — anything with a shape
reads better with one, and a shadow is usually the difference between a caption
that sits *on* the picture and one that sits *in front of* it.

**It needs a shape to work with.** A layer that fills the frame edge to edge has
no silhouette, so there is nothing to cast and you will see nothing. Put it on a
title, a shape layer, a keyed subject, or a footage layer that has been masked
or scaled down.

## What each control does

- **Shadow colour** — what the shadow is made of. Black is the default and
  usually right, but a shadow is really *the absence of a light*, so tinting it
  towards the colour opposite your key light is what makes it look considered
  rather than stuck on. The range runs past white so a bright "shadow" is
  typable, which is how a glow-behind is faked in one instance.
- **Opacity** — how much of that colour lands. 50 is the default because a
  shadow at full strength reads as a hole rather than as a shadow.
- **Direction** — where the light is, in degrees. **Measured from straight up,
  turning clockwise**: 0° puts the shadow directly above the layer, 90° to the
  right, 135° (the default) down and to the right, which is where almost every
  interface in the world puts it.
- **Distance** — how far the shadow slides. Small distances read as "resting
  just above the background", large ones as "floating well in front of it".
- **Softness** — how blurred the shadow is. It is independent of Distance on
  purpose, so you can move a shadow without changing how sharp it is. In real
  life the two travel together — a light further away casts a sharper shadow
  further off — so animating both is what sells a lift.
- **Shadow only** — keeps the shadow and throws the layer away. This is how you
  put a shadow on a *different* layer from the thing casting it: duplicate the
  layer, put Shadow only on the copy, and move the copy anywhere in the stack.
- **Mix** — how much of the whole result replaces the original. Below 100 the
  shadow fades; at 0 nothing happens at all, which is where a fade-in starts.
- **Matte** and **Invert** — drive the effect from another layer's brightness;
  see [Effects](/use/effects/).

## Notes

**The shadow can leave the frame, and that is correct.** A shape touching the
frame border casts a shadow that goes off the edge; those pixels are simply not
there. Holding the border pixel outward instead would smear it into a fan, which
reads as a fault.

**Several shadows are several instances.** Two Drop shadows at different
distances and opacities is the standard recipe for a soft ambient shadow plus a
tight contact one, and it stacks in the order you would expect: the lower one in
the list is drawn further back.
