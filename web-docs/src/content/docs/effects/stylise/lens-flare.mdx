Lens flare is a [Stylise](/effects/stylise/) effect.

## What it is for

A **simulated** lens flare rather than a stack of drawn sprites. The ghosts are
traced through a real lens prescription — the actual element curvatures, glasses
and spacings of a photographic lens — so they scale, stretch, colour and slide
across the frame exactly as a camera's do. The starburst is the true diffraction
pattern of the aperture.

That is what you get for the cost. A sprite-based flare fakes the ghost train
with drawn ellipses, so every light looks the same and nothing responds to the
lens. Here, opening the aperture makes the ghosts grow and go round; stopping it
down makes them small and bladed. Moving the light across frame flips the train
through the optical centre. The blue, green and magenta casts come from each
element's anti-reflective coating, the same way they do in life.

It is the most expensive effect Lumit ships, and it owns a render pass of its
own. Use **Quality** and **Detail** to buy it back while you work.

Choose your **Lens** first. The twenty in the library were curated to have
maximally different flare characters — modern multicoated cine glass, uncoated
1930s exotics, superspeeds, process glass, long telephotos — so switching between
them changes the look far more than any of the other controls will. You can also
point **Lens file** at your own prescription.

The whole thing is a pure function of its parameters, so it renders identically
every time, and a preview matches an export.

### When it is doing too much

In order: pull **Intensity** down, then **Ghost intensity** and
**Starburst intensity** to weigh the two halves against each other, then
**Max ghosts** to thin the train, then **Quality** if it is the cost rather than
the look, and **Mix** as the final blend.

## What each control does

The light, and how strong the whole thing is:

- **Light x** and **Light y** — where the light sits, in pixels. Open past the
  frame's edges: a light off-screen still flares. There is a dropper for picking
  it straight off the Viewer.
- **Source width** and **Source height** — the half-size of the emitting area. At
  zero the light is a point, which is what a distant lamp or the sun is. Give it a
  size and it becomes an **area** light whose ghosts take the shape of the source
  rather than of a point — which is what a window, a strip light or a practical
  actually is, and it changes the ghost train considerably.
- **Intensity** — the master gain on everything the effect adds. Zero adds
  nothing.
- **F-stop** — stops the iris down from the lens's own widest aperture. Wide open,
  the ghosts are large and round; stopped down they shrink and take on the shape
  of the blades. This is the single most photographic control here.
- **Lens** — the prescription being traced. Changing it changes everything about
  the flare's character, so try a few before adjusting anything else.
- **Lens file** — your own prescription, in the same format the optical bench
  tools use. Set, it overrides the Lens choice entirely; unset, missing or
  unreadable, it falls back to the picked lens rather than failing.

The **Lens options** group is the glass and the iris:

- **Focus (m)** — the distance the lens is focused at. Refocusing shifts the
  sensor plane and visibly rearranges the whole ghost train — the same lens at a
  different focus is a different flare.
- **Anamorphic squeeze** — the horizontal squeeze of an anamorphic lens, which
  stretches the flare into the familiar wide oval.
- **Blades** — how many blades the iris has, and so how many sides a stopped-down
  ghost and how many spikes the starburst have.
- **Rotation** — turns the iris. It winds through whole turns, so it can be
  animated to spin.
- **Coating** — how well the elements are anti-reflection coated overall.
  Uncoated glass gives bright, neutral ghosts, which is the look of pre-war
  lenses; fully coated gives dim, strongly colour-cast ones, which is modern
  glass.
- **Roundness** and **Softness** — the iris opening's shape and the softness of
  its edge.
- **Element 1** through **Element 20** — the coating on each individual piece of
  glass. Only as many rows appear as the chosen lens actually has elements. Left
  as the lens file says, each row changes nothing; set one and you override that
  element's coating, which is how you push a particular ghost's colour without
  disturbing the rest of the train.

The **Flare options** group shapes the result:

- **Ghost intensity** — the gain on the ghost train alone.
- **Ghost softness** — a touch of out-of-focus blur on the ghosts, as a share of
  the frame diagonal. Zero is a clean, sharp setting rather than a broken one;
  this is taste, not a cover for artefacts.
- **Max ghosts** — how many of the ghosts render. They are ranked by brightness
  and the cap drops the dimmest first, so lowering it thins the train from the
  faint end rather than cutting it arbitrarily.
- **Detail** — buys more or less tracing work than the Quality tier alone would
  spend, without changing tier. Raise it when a ghost's rim still shows structure;
  lower it for a cheaper preview.
- **Dispersion** — how far the traced wavelengths spread apart. At zero the trace
  is monochrome and there is no colour fringing at all; at the neutral point it
  is physically correct, and above that it is exaggerated.
- **Starburst intensity** — the gain on the diffraction spikes alone.
- **Scale** — scales the whole flare about the optical centre, ghosts and
  starburst together.

The **Source** group decides what the lights *are*:

- **Source** — Manual light is the tracked-point workflow: one source at the Light
  position. Matte finds the flare's sources in a layer's own picture, so
  practicals and highlights in the footage each throw their own flare. Lights
  takes the composition's own light layers as the sources: every light that is
  visible and alive at that moment throws a flare from its own position, at its
  own animated intensity, so lighting the scene and flaring it become one job. A
  composition with no light layers flares with nothing in this mode — it does not
  fall back to the Light position, which would put a flare somewhere nobody asked
  for.
- **Light tint** — multiplies every light in every mode. In Manual mode it is
  simply the flare's colour. It costs nothing to animate.
- **Use source colour** — in Matte and Lights modes, whether each source flares in
  its own colour, so a warm practical flares warm and a cool one cool. Turned
  off, every source flares white through the tint alone, which is what you want
  when the sources are being used purely as a set of positions. The row appears
  only in those two modes, since a Manual flare has just the one colour anyway.
- **Matte layer** — which layer is searched for sources. It defaults to the layer
  the effect is on, because "flare the lights in this picture" is nearly always
  what asking for a matte source means. On an adjustment layer that is the
  composite of everything below. Point it at another layer and that layer is
  rendered on its own, and is expected to be hidden.
- **Threshold** — how bright a pixel must be to count as a light, in scene-linear
  brightness. At the top of the slider only genuinely over-range highlights
  qualify; lower it and ordinary bright picture starts to flare too.
- **Threshold softness** — how gradually sources fade in as they cross the
  threshold. Widen it if lights pop in and out as the footage moves.

And the tail:

- **Quality** — the cost ladder, in four tiers. It sets how many wavelengths are
  traced and how dense the ray grid is, which is what separates a smooth spectral
  fringe from a stack of coloured copies. Draft also traces the flare at half
  resolution, which makes it the one for scrubbing rather than for looking at.
  Normal stands on its own for finished work; High and Ultra buy a cleaner fringe
  and smoother ghost rims on the shots that need them, at a cost that climbs
  steeply. Reach for Detail above before jumping a tier.
- **Blend** — how the flare combines with the layer beneath it, the same question a
  layer's own mode asks. Everything the effect draws is a black-backed light
  element, so Add is the natural choice and the default. **Normal** is the odd one
  out: the element *replaces* the layer, black background and all, which gives
  you the flare on opaque black to export for a separate compositing pass.
- **Mix** — how much of the flared result replaces the original.
