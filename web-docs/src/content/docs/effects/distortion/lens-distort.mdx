Lens distort is a **Distortion** effect.

## What it is for

Bending the picture the way a real lens does — or unbending it. A wide lens bows
straight lines outward: the wider it is, the more a horizon curves and the more
the corners stretch. This effect adds that bow, and with **Reverse** on it takes
the same bow away.

Both directions get used constantly:

- **Removing it.** Footage from an action camera or a phone ultra-wide has
  visible barrel distortion. Straighten it before tracking or before adding a
  graphic, or the graphic will slide against the plate.
- **Adding it.** A clean render, a title, or a screen replacement dropped onto
  distorted footage looks pasted on because it is too straight. Give it the same
  field of view as the plate and it sits into the shot.

The control is an angle rather than an abstract strength, because a lens has a
field of view written on it. At Orientation Horizontal, 40° means "the width of
this frame sees 40° of the world" — the number you can look up for the lens the
shot was taken on.

## What each control does

- **Field of view** — how wide the lens is, in degrees, across whichever side
  **Orientation** names. 0 does nothing at all. Around 30–50° is a normal lens
  and bends gently; 90° and beyond is the action-camera look, with the corners
  visibly pulled in. It is the only strength control there is: everything else
  says where and which way.
- **Reverse** — off adds the bow (barrel: straight lines bow outward, more of the
  world squeezed into the frame); on removes it (pincushion: lines bow inward).
  The two are exact opposites, so a Lens distort and a reversed Lens distort at
  the same Field of view, Orientation and Centre hand the picture back.
- **Orientation** — which side of the frame the angle spans: the **width**
  (Horizontal, the usual meaning of a lens specification), the **height**, or the
  **diagonal**. On a wide frame the same number bends much harder at Vertical
  than at Horizontal, so this is not a cosmetic choice.
- **Centre X** and **Centre Y** — the optical centre the bend is about. Usually
  the middle of the frame; move it when the plate was shot on a shifted lens, or
  when it has been cropped off centre.
- **Edges** — what the corners read when the bend pulls in content from outside
  the frame. **Transparent** is honest and is the default. **Repeat** holds the
  border pixel outward, which fills the corner with a streak — sometimes usable
  under a vignette. **Mirror** folds the picture back on itself, which fills the
  corner with plausible content and is the best cheat when the corners must not
  be empty.
- **Mix** — how much of the bent picture replaces the original. Blending the two
  shows both, so use Field of view rather than Mix to bend less.
- **Matte** and **Invert** — drive the effect from another layer's brightness;
  see [Effects](/use/effects/).

## Notes

**Match the plate, then check a straight line.** The practical workflow is to put
a straight edge in the shot — a door frame, a horizon, a temporary guide layer —
turn Reverse on, and raise Field of view until the line is straight. Whatever
number you land on is the plate's, and any element you need to marry into the
shot gets the same number with Reverse off.

**The layer does not get bigger.** Removing distortion pulls the picture inward
and leaves the corners empty; Lumit renders effects at the frame's own size and
has no per-effect resize. If you need the recovered corners, scale the layer down
inside a larger composition first.

**After Effects users:** this is the counterpart of Optics Compensation, and its
Field of View, Reverse Lens Distortion, FOV Orientation and View Center all map
across. AE's Resize / Optimal Pixels has no equivalent, for the reason above.
