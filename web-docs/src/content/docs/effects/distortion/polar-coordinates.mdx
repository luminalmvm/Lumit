Polar coordinates is a **Distortion** effect.

## What it is for

Turning straight things into round things, and round things into straight ones.

**Rectangular to polar** wraps the picture around the middle of the frame: the top
row of the picture becomes a tiny circle at the centre, the bottom row becomes the
outermost ring, and the left and right edges meet in a seam pointing straight up.
It is how a panorama becomes a "tiny planet", how a bar of light becomes a halo,
and how a scrolling strip of text becomes a spinning ring.

**Polar to rectangular** is the exact opposite: it unrolls whatever surrounds the
centre into a flat strip. Point it at a circular logo, a clock face or a
[Radial wipe](/effects/transition/radial-wipe/) and you get its contents laid out
left to right by angle.

## What each control does

- **Conversion** — which way the picture is bent. The two are exact opposites of
  one another: put a Rectangular to polar and a Polar to rectangular on top of each
  other, both at Interpolation 100, and you get the picture back.
- **Interpolation** — how far along the bend the effect has gone. This is not a
  fade. At 50 the frame is genuinely *half bent* — every pixel is drawn from
  half-way along its own journey into the circle — so keyframing it from 0 to 100
  makes the picture curl up rather than dissolving into a curled copy of itself.
  It is the control to animate.
- **Mix** — the ordinary blend of the finished result over the original. At 50 you
  see the bent picture *and* the flat one at once, which is a different look and
  occasionally the one you want.
- **Matte** and **Invert** — drive the effect from another layer's brightness;
  see [Effects](/use/effects/).

## Notes

**The seam points straight up.** The left and right edges of the picture end up
next to each other at twelve o'clock. If the join shows, put an
[Offset](/effects/distortion/offset/) above the Polar coordinates and slide the
picture sideways until the two edges match, or make the source seamless in the
first place.

**The middle is where detail goes to die.** In Rectangular to polar the entire top
row of the picture is squeezed onto a handful of pixels at the centre. That is
inherent to the transform, not a shortcoming: put something plain — sky, a
gradient, a solid colour — along the top of your source and the centre of the
planet will look clean.

**Corners are covered, so the frame stays full.** The wrapping radius reaches the
frame's corners rather than only its edges, which is why a tiny planet fills the
frame instead of leaving four bald corners. The cost is that the very bottom of
the source is seen only near the corners.

**Unrolling can run out of picture.** Polar to rectangular reads from a circle
wider than the frame is tall, so parts of the strip have nothing to read and come
out transparent. Put the content you care about near the centre of frame, or scale
it down first.
