Set matte is a **Utility** effect.

## What it is for

Giving one layer the shape of another. Pick a layer in the **Matte** row, pick
which of its channels to read, and that channel becomes this layer's
transparency — so a title takes the shape of a cloud, a colour fill takes the
shape of a gradient, or a video takes the shape of a logo.

It is the everyday tool for building a matte out of something that was never
meant to be one. A [Fractal noise](/effects/generate/fractal-noise/) layer
becomes an organic dissolve. A [Gradient](/effects/generate/gradient/) becomes a
falloff. A luma pass out of a game capture becomes a mask nobody had to draw.

**Its Matte row means something different from every other effect's.** On the
rest of the catalogue, the Matte row is a *strength*: the effect runs everywhere
and is faded back where the matte is dark. Here the matte **is** the answer —
the channel you choose becomes the coverage, full stop. The difference is easy
to see: point a strength matte at a white disc and you get the whole frame with
a faint ring in it; point Set matte at the same disc and you get a disc.

## What each control does

- **Matte** — the layer whose channel becomes this layer's transparency. Until
  you pick one the effect does nothing at all, which is the honest behaviour: a
  layer you have to supply cannot have a sensible default. It is rendered on its
  own at this composition's size, so nothing needs aligning or stretching, and
  the **Layer source** dropdown beside it decides whether you read that layer's
  raw content, its masks, or its finished picture with its own effects applied.
- **Invert** — read the matte the other way round: dark where it was light. This
  is the switch you reach for about half the time, since a matte drawn as "the
  bit I want" and one drawn as "the bit I want gone" look identical until you
  try one.
- **Channel** — which number in the matte layer carries the shape.
  **Luminance** (the default) is the brightness of the picture, which is what
  you want from a greyscale pass, a noise or a ramp. **Alpha** is the matte
  layer's own transparency, which is what you want when the matte is itself a
  shape or a keyed subject. **Red**, **Green** and **Blue** read one channel
  alone — useful when someone has packed three separate masks into one image,
  which is common in game and 3D output.
- **Combine with existing alpha** — intersect instead of replace. Off, the layer
  takes the matte's shape outright and forgets its own. On, it keeps its own
  edge and is *further* cut by the matte, so a shape that was already soft stays
  soft. Reach for it when the layer arrived with transparency worth keeping.
- **Mix** — how much of the new transparency takes hold. Animating it from 0 to
  100 is a dissolve *into* the matte's shape, which is a nicer reveal than a
  plain fade.

## Notes

**It changes coverage, not colour.** The effect works on straight (unmultiplied)
values, so making a pixel half-transparent does not also make it half as bright.
That sounds obvious and is exactly what goes wrong when it is done the other way:
edges darken and thin.

**A matte pointed at this layer reads this layer.** Point the row at the layer
the effect is on and it does not render a second copy — it reads the picture as
it stands at that point in the stack. On an adjustment layer that is everything
underneath, which is how you cut a whole stack of layers to one shape at once.

**Set matte or a track matte?** A track matte (in the timeline) is the right tool
when one layer's whole job is to shape the layer below it. Set matte is the right
tool when the shaping is one step inside a stack of effects — before a glow, say,
or after a grade — because it happens exactly where you put it.
