Noise is a **Generate** effect.

## What it is for

Sprinkling light and dark over the picture, one pixel at a time.

Turned up it is video noise, a deliberate wall of static. Turned down — five or
ten per cent — it is film grain, and that is what it is usually for: a small
amount of grain over a computer-made image stops it looking computer-made. It is
the standard cure for a flat sky or a gentle gradient breaking into visible
bands, and it is what makes a clean graphic sit convincingly on top of shot
footage, which has grain of its own.

Unlike the rest of this family it does not replace the picture — it works on
whatever arrived. It is also the only one of the four whose result changes over
time on its own.

## What each control does

- **Amount** — how far a pixel may be pushed, as a percentage of full scale. 100
  means a pixel can move by the whole distance from black to white, which is
  static; single figures are grain. The slider stops at 100 but larger numbers
  can be typed.
- **Distribution** — **Uniform** picks any value in the range with equal odds, so
  the grain is even and slightly harsh. **Gaussian** clusters values near zero,
  so most pixels move a little and few move a lot, which is what real film grain
  does and what you want under about twenty per cent.
- **Colour noise** — off, all three channels move together, so the grain is
  purely light and dark and does not tint anything. On, each channel is drawn
  separately, which gives the coloured speckle of a badly lit video camera.
- **Animate** — on, a fresh pattern every frame, which is what grain does. Off,
  one pattern frozen in place, which is what a texture does. A frozen pattern
  over moving footage reads as dirt on the lens rather than grain in the stock.
- **Seed** — which random pattern this instance follows. Two Noise effects with
  the same seed produce the same grain; change one of them if they are meant to
  look independent.
- **Mix** — how much of the grained picture replaces the original.
- **Matte** and **Invert** — drive the effect from another layer's brightness;
  see [Effects](/use/effects/). Pointing the Matte at a luminance ramp is the way
  to put grain in the shadows and leave the highlights clean.

## Notes

**Nothing is clipped.** After Effects offers a switch to keep the result inside
the 0–100 range because it works in a range that has a ceiling. Lumit works in
scene-linear light, which has headroom, so grain rides on top of a highlight
instead of flattening it — and can push a dark pixel slightly below zero, which
the compositor handles and later effects can recover from.

**Grain is applied before the layer's transparency**, so it lies evenly over the
picture rather than fading out across a soft edge along with the coverage.
