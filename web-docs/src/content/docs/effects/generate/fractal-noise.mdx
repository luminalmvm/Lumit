Fractal noise is a **Generate** effect.

## What it is for

Making a repeatable pattern of soft blobs, with smaller blobs laid over the big
ones, and smaller ones over those. That description sounds thin and the effect is
not: clouds, smoke, mist, water caustics, grunge and rust, fire, dust, organic
wipes, and — above all — the maps that drive *other* effects are all this one
picture with different numbers in it.

Two ideas do most of the work.

**Layers of detail.** One layer of noise is a lava lamp. Six layers, each half
the size and a bit fainter than the one before, is a cloud, because that is how
real surfaces are built: big shapes with finer shapes on them all the way down.
**Complexity** is how many layers, **Sub influence** how much each one counts
for, **Sub scaling** how much smaller each one is.

**Depth, not reshuffling.** The pattern is three-dimensional and you are looking
at a slice of it. **Evolution** moves the slice through the third dimension, so
animating it makes the pattern churn and drift the way smoke does, rather than
flickering into an unrelated pattern every frame. One full turn of the dial
moves one step deeper.

The most valuable use is as a **matte source**. Put a Fractal noise on a solid,
point another effect's Matte row at that solid, and the effect now varies across
the frame with a shape no slider could describe — a blur that is soft in patches,
a grade that moves like weather. That is what half of the After Effects world is
built out of, and it is why this landed before the distortion effects that read
it.

## What each control does

- **Noise type** — **Perlin** is the smooth, rounded, cloud-like pattern most
  people picture. **Value** is the cheaper cousin: the same idea built from
  corner values rather than corner slopes, which gives a slightly squarer, more
  woven look. Perlin for clouds, Value when you want the grid to show a little.
- **Fractal type** — **Basic** adds the layers as they are, giving soft billows.
  **Turbulent** folds each layer about its middle first, which turns the smooth
  midtones into creases and gives the ropey, smoke-and-flame look. Turbulent is
  the default and is usually what a displacement map wants.
- **Invert noise** — flips the finished pattern, light for dark, after Contrast
  and Brightness. (The **Invert** further down the panel is the matte's, not
  this one's.)
- **Contrast** — how hard the pattern pushes toward black and white. At 0 it
  flattens to grey; at 100 it is left alone; above that the midtones squeeze out
  and the pattern breaks into distinct light and dark regions, which is how a
  cloud becomes a hard-edged organic wipe.
- **Brightness** — lifts or lowers the whole pattern after Contrast. Between them
  these two are the aim on any matte use: raise Contrast until the shapes are
  crisp, then set Brightness to decide how much of the frame is white.
- **Rotation** — turns the pattern under the frame. Useful when Scale width and
  Scale height have stretched it into streaks and the streaks need to run
  diagonally.
- **Uniform scaling** — on, one **Scale** sizes the pattern both ways. Off,
  **Scale width** and **Scale height** size it separately, which is how a cloud
  becomes horizontal streaks or vertical rain.
- **Scale**, **Scale width**, **Scale height** — the size of one blob, in pixels
  at composition size. This is a real length, not a percentage of something
  unnamed: 200 means roughly a 200-pixel blob on any composition, at any preview
  resolution.
- **Offset x** and **Offset y** — where the pattern sits. Animate them to drift
  the whole field across the frame, which combined with Evolution gives cloud
  that both travels and churns.
- **Complexity** — how many layers of detail. 1 is smooth blobs; 6 is a cloud;
  10 is as fine as it goes. Each extra layer costs time, so leave it low if the
  fine detail is going to be blurred away anyway.
- **Sub influence** — how loud each layer is compared with the one before, as a
  percentage. Low values leave the big shapes dominant and the result smooth;
  100 makes every layer equally loud, which is noise rather than a surface.
- **Sub scaling** — how much smaller each layer is than the one before. 50 is the
  textbook halving; the default 55 is a shade gentler, which is where After
  Effects sits and where clouds look most natural.
- **Evolution** — the depth dial. Keyframe it to animate the pattern. One full
  turn moves one step, so a slow drift is a few turns over the length of a shot;
  spinning it fast makes the pattern boil.
- **Cycle evolution** and **Cycle** — make Evolution loop. With Cycle at 3, the
  pattern at 0 turns and the pattern at 3 turns are the *same picture*, so
  animating Evolution from 0 to 1080° gives a clip that tiles end to end with no
  seam. The loop is exact at every Complexity.
- **Seed** — which pattern this instance draws. Two Fractal noises with the same
  settings and different seeds are two different clouds.
- **Mix** — how much of the pattern replaces the original picture.
- **Matte** and **Invert** — drive the effect from another layer's brightness;
  see [Effects](/use/effects/).

## Notes

**It fills the whole frame, opaquely.** The layer's own transparency is not
consulted, so a Fractal noise on a shape does not take the shape. Put it on a
solid and shape it with a mask, or drive it with a Matte.

**The pattern is the same on every machine and every run**, which is what makes
it usable at all: an export today and an export next year are identical to the
bit, and the preview you graded against is the frame you get.

**A note on scale and After Effects.** After Effects gives Scale as a percentage
of a size it never names, so the same number means different things on different
compositions. Here it is a length, which survives a resize and a change of
preview resolution. An imported Fractal Noise has its percentage converted, and
the import reports it as a converted value rather than an exact one.
