**Retime** maps a layer's time (or clip time, inside a Sequence layer). Use it
to ramp speed or hold a frame. It can also run footage backwards.

## What can be retimed

- **Footage layers** have their own Retime.
- **Precomp layers** have their own Retime.
- Inside a [**Sequence layer**](/use/sequence-layers/), each cut clip has its Retime.

## Speed

**Speed** is the rate the retime map runs at:

| Speed | Result |
| --- | --- |
| 100% | Normal. |
| 0% | A **freeze**. The frame holds. |
| −100% | Reverse. |

Speed is easiest to work with through the **Speed** lens of the graph editor rather than
the Value lens, which shows which moment of the source is on screen instead.

## Make a speed ramp

<Shot file="speed-ramp.png" caption="A speed ramp in the graph editor, seen through the Speed lens."/>

1. Turn on Retime for the layer.
2. Add a keyframe where the ramp starts.
3. Add another where it ends.
4. Shape the curve between them in [the graph editor](/use/graph-editor/).

## Overrun

**Overrunning** is when the map asks for source time past the end of the media, or before its
start. Lumit clamps the boundary frames and marks the region in the timeline.

Overrun never moves layer boundaries or edit points.

## Frame interpolation

When a retime lands between two source frames, Lumit has to decide what to show. The
**In-between frames** setting on a footage layer's Source card offers two answers:

| Mode | How |
| --- | --- |
| **Nearest** | Repeat the closer frame. Sharp, but it stutters. |
| **Blend** | Crossfade the two frames. Smooth, but it ghosts. |

The third answer is **Flow**, which measures the motion between the two frames and
synthesises the one in between. It is the **Flow** switch in the layer's switch cluster
rather than an entry in that dropdown, because it is the most expensive thing a layer can
ask for and it carries controls of its own. Turning it on reveals a **Flow** group in the
Effect Controls panel: measurement resolution, input rate, vector detail, smoothness, how
occlusions are handled, what to fall back to, a guard for on-screen overlays, and a switch
to force it on where it would otherwise stand down.

Turning Flow off returns the layer to Nearest and parks the group's settings, so comparing
a flow shot against the plain one costs nothing.

Frame interpolation is a render setting either way. It does not change the retime map.

## Stretch

**Stretch** asks the question the other way round: not "what does the curve do", but "play
this at 50% and give it the length that implies". Right-click the layer's row in the
Timeline and choose **Stretch…**.

The dialogue takes a **new speed** in per cent or a **new duration**, and editing either
well moves the other — they are one number seen twice. At half speed a layer is twice as
long, and a montage is usually fighting you about one of the two rather than the other.

The layer is anchored at its **in point**: it starts where it started, and its end moves.
The frame showing at the in point is the frame that stays there.

Stretch is not a second rate multiplier hiding behind the map. It rescales the Retime map
itself, so the graph editor goes on showing the true curve, and an existing shape is kept —
every key's time is scaled about the in point and its eased sides go with it, which is the
same shape said over a longer or shorter stretch. A layer with no map yet gets the plain
one first, so stretching an ordinary layer means what it looks like it means. One undo
step: the length and the map move together or not at all.

A **clip** inside a Sequence layer has **Speed…** instead, and no duration well. A clip's
place and length are fixed — re-speeding one never moves its edges — so a duration to type
would be a promise Lumit is right to refuse. **Reset speed** on the same menu puts it back
to 100%.

## Freeze a frame where you are

Right-click the layer's row and choose **Freeze frame at playhead**. The moment showing is
held for one second, everything after it is pushed that far later, and the map is cropped
back to the layer's own out point — so the layer's length never changes and anything cut to
the beat stays cut to the beat. The tail may newly overrun, and that is drawn rather than
quietly repaired.

The hold is two ordinary keyframes once it is made, so it drags and eases like anything
else. It is refused on a Sequence layer, whose clips carry their own maps, and at a moment
on or outside the layer's own ends, where there is nothing to split.

## The razor through a ramp

A [Sequence layer's](/use/sequence-layers/) razor cuts straight through an eased speed
ramp. The two halves' maps, laid end to end, are the curve that was there before — not
close to it, the same — so each half goes on playing the source moment it was playing, and
neither edge moves.
