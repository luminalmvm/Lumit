Lumit tracks in two ways, and they answer different questions. [Camera
track](/effects/utility/camera-track/) works out **where the camera was**, and hands you a
Camera layer that moves the way the real one did. [Planar
track](/effects/utility/planar-track/) works out **where one flat thing is** — a phone
screen, a sign, a poster — and hands you a [Corner pin](/effects/distortion/corner-pin/)
keyed to it.

Both are effects, applied to the layer holding the shot, and both keep working while you
carry on editing.

## Camera track

1. Select the footage layer and apply **Effect ▸ Utility ▸ Camera track**.
2. Press **Analyse**.
3. Wait. The status row under the buttons counts the frames as they go, and **Cancel**
   stops it.

The analysis is of the **whole source clip**, not of the layer you applied it to, and it is
kept beside the project. Every clip using that footage shares the one solve, so trimming a
clip, moving it, reordering it or [retiming](/use/retime/) it never tracks anything again.
Re-analysing is only needed when you change one of the settings.

| Control | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Feature density** | How many points are followed. More is slower but copes better with hard footage. |
| **Use masks** | Leaves the layer's masks out of the track. Use it for anything moving on its own, like a person walking through. |
| **Show points** | Draws the solved points over the picture. |

### A track that stops part-way

A shot can stop being followable. The lens racks, the frame whites out, there is a cut in
the middle of the file — and past that point there is nothing that can be tied to anything
before it. Lumit stops there and solves the part that worked rather than inventing the rest.

A thin bar above the status line shows the analysed span against the length of the clip, and
the line reads *Analysed 180 of 400 frames — the shot could not be followed further*. It
appears once the work is over and does not move: it measures how far the answer reaches, not
how far the work has got.

A camera following a partial track moves inside the span and holds its last position past
the end. Mask out whatever broke the shot and analyse again, or treat the two halves as two
shots.

If nothing could be solved at all, the line says why in a sentence — the media could not be
read, too little in the shot stayed still enough to follow, the camera only turned so there
is no depth to recover.

### The camera

**Create camera** beside the status makes a [Camera layer](/use/camera/) that follows the
solve. Its Transform heading wears a *Following the solve* badge, and its position and zoom
are derived from the shot on every frame.

Past the end of what was solved the badge reads *Holding the last solved frame*. If the
tracked layer is deleted or its media goes offline it reads *The solve could not be found*
and the camera holds where it was — never a silent freeze.

### Nudging it

A solve is a measurement, and measurements are a little wrong. A ground plane half a degree
off is normal, and re-solving is not a fix for it.

So a linked camera's own transform and zoom rows still take edits, and what you type there
is a **correction**: the difference from where the rows sat when the link was made, added on
top of the solved motion, on every frame. Ordinary rows, ordinary keyframes, ordinary undo —
you already know how to key a camera.

A small dot beside the badge, and on the Camera track's own status row, says the camera has
been nudged. **Clear corrections** puts the seven rows back where they started and leaves the
link alone. **Convert to keyframes** bakes the whole thing — solve and correction together,
which is the motion that was on screen — into one key per frame and ends the link. From then
on it is an ordinary camera.

### The point cloud

With **Show points** on, the solved points draw over the picture on the tracked layer, cued
by depth. Select some — singly, or by sweeping a box round them — and two buttons appear
over the picture: **Create null** and **Create solid**, each placed at the average position
of what you picked and facing the camera. That is how you get something into the scene at
the right depth.

## Planar track

A flat surface filmed by a moving camera always warps the same way, however the camera moves
and however the surface turns. Four corners describe it completely, which is why the answer
to a planar track is a Corner pin.

1. Select the footage layer and apply **Effect ▸ Utility ▸ Planar track**.
2. Put the effect's four points round the flat thing, on the clip's **first frame**. That
   frame is the reference, and every later frame is measured against it.
3. Mask out anything that crosses the surface — a hand over the phone. The layer's own masks
   still apply inside the quad.
4. Press **Analyse**.
5. Set **Pin layer** to the layer you want pinned, and press **Create corner pin**.

The Corner pin is appended to that layer's stack with one keyframe per frame of its own
extent. They are ordinary keyframes: the [graph editor](/panels/graph-editor/) draws them,
undo takes them back, and you can soften a corner or trim the last ten frames afterwards
without anything resolving behind you. A layer that already carries a Corner pin keeps it —
you asked for a pin, not for a tidy-up.

**Feature density** and **Use masks** work as they do on the Camera track.

### Reading the status

*Surface tracked over 400 frames* is a whole track. *Tracked 180 of 400 frames* means the
surface stopped being followable, and the span bar shows how far it reached.

A second line may read *Re-anchored twice, so the far end may drift a little*. Every frame
is measured against the reference frame rather than against the frame before it, which is
what stops a planar track walking off its surface. When too few of the reference frame's
features survive to trust the fit, the measurement adopts a recent frame instead and carries
a little error forward. Zero re-anchors is a track with no accumulated drift in it at all,
and the count is the one number that says how much to trust the far end.

*What is inside the shape is not one flat surface* means the four points enclose something
that is not flat, or whose parts move against each other.

:::note[Limitations]
The quad is four rows in the panel; dragging its corners on the picture is not built. The
quad cannot be keyframed — it is the shape the surface has on the reference frame, and
animating it would be asking the tracker to follow a moving target from a moving start. And
the Planar track analyses a footage layer only, where the Camera track will also analyse a
Precomp layer by rendering it.

Point tracking that produces a keyframed transform — the third thing a tracker is usually
asked for — is not built either.
:::
